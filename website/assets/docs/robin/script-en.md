# ROBIN: Negotiating Control

**A video essay / micro-documentary** — production script (English)

> This script accompanies the short film *ROBIN: Negotiating Control*. It doubles as the film's production document and as a standalone written piece.
>
> **Runtime:** 4:45–5:05
> **Thesis:** To control a complex intention, we must first write it as an executable rule. The rule is never the intention itself. Creativity sometimes lives in how we hear that gap, and in taking responsibility for the next move.
> **Evidence:** the compressor's audible transformation; a target formalized into a metric; Robin's mapping rules; and a live performance interpreted in real time.

---

## 0. Working Principles

- This is a video essay / micro-documentary, not a product demo of Robin, and not a pure atmosphere piece. Every section pushes the same question: **how much of creation can a person control?**
- The compressor is the opening piece of evidence. "An intention written as a rule" is the conceptual bridge in the middle. Robin is the core evidence. Section 2 does not expand into social critique; it only establishes the problem in 25–30 seconds.
- Robin is not a "random instrument." It is a bounded system: the performer decides the sound pool, the proxy rules, and the actions; the system decides part of the concrete outcome; the performer responds to the outcome.
- **Anchor of honesty.** "Live and unedited" is no longer proven by an unbroken wide shot. It is anchored by three things:
  1. The performance audio is a single continuous, unedited take. The image may be montaged; the sound may not jump.
  2. The real-time visualization carries a continuous timeline, so the viewer can see that time is never cut.
  3. The causality of key actions stays visible: for every system response, the viewer first sees the action (touch / movement), then hears the sound.
- **The cutting rate is the fourth language.** The cut rate itself is a dramaturgical parameter: D1–D2 cut slowly, D3 begins to mix, D4 accelerates, D5 reaches the film's peak (high-frequency montage), and D6 settles into a near, long take. The chaos is expressed through editing density, not through sonic failure.
- The performance may be abstract, but the viewer must never have to guess the rules. Interpretation happens simultaneously with the performance: between an action, the system's response, and the next choice, a very short sound, text, or state readout helps the viewer read the causality. There is no long after-the-fact recap once the performance ends.

## 2. Detailed Script

### Section 1. Hook: I wanted to control a single drum hit (0:00–0:35)

**Image / sound**

- Black screen. First, one unprocessed drum hit with clear dynamic range.
- Close-up: a compressor in the DAW; a hand turning the threshold down. The screen splits: the original waveform / sound on the left, the compressed sound on the right.
- The same groove plays a second time. It is more even, more forward now; under a more aggressive setting, audible saturation or harmonic coloration remains.
- End on the knob and the compressed tail. Title card: **ROBIN — Negotiating Control**.

**Narration (VO)**

> All I wanted was to control a single drum hit.
> Make the loud parts quieter. Let the soft parts be heard.
> So I used a compressor.
> It made the dynamics manageable. But the harder I pushed it, the more the sound changed: denser, harder, taking on colors it never had before.
> I controlled its volume. But not only its volume.

**Production notes**

- Record a comparable A/B audio pair; never just photograph the plugin interface.
- Do not claim "all compressors produce harmonics." Instead: "under this aggressive setting, I heard…" Keep it accurate.

### Section 2. Intention Written as a Rule: Control Creates a New Game (0:35–1:05)

**Image / sound**

- Freeze the compressor as two lines of on-screen text: `I want: to keep the drum's character` and `I write down: narrow the dynamic range`.
- The second line gradually compresses into a single meter / score bar. As the score rises, the drum's color keeps changing.
- A concrete word stamps over the bar: `to learn` becomes `to score`. This is the film's only classroom image, a bridge showing a rule executed too faithfully, not an argument about education.
- An abstract animation: `complex intention → measurable rule → optimized result → what was left out?`
- The compressed drum keeps playing; a short tail escapes the tidy loop.

**Narration (VO)**

> In AI research, there's a phenomenon called specification gaming.
> When a goal is too complex, we write it down as a rule that can be measured and executed.
> The system works hard to fulfill that rule. What it optimizes is the written metric, not what the person actually wanted.
> I'd actually seen this long before, in a classroom: we wrote "to learn well" as "to score well," and everything began to revolve around grades.
> The system didn't break the rule. It just executed the rule too faithfully.
> Every act of control creates a new game.

**The question this section leaves**

> If expression itself must be written as inputs, data, and rules, then what does the rule leave out?

**Editing boundaries**

- Do not add games, schools, hospitals, companies as extra examples; they would turn a film about music into a collage of social issues. The classroom image appears once, for one sentence, and the film returns to music.
- Do not say Robin "reward hacks." Robin is not an AI that self-optimizes a reward. This film borrows the broader claim: **formal rule ≠ human intention**.

### Section 3. Where Robin Came From: I Didn't Hide the Loss of Translation (1:05–1:40)

**Image / sound**

- In sequence: photos of soldering Robin; close-ups of the eleven pads; the accelerometer; Tone.js code in a browser; both hands picking Robin up.
- Brief factual labels: `11 touch sensors`, `binary velocity`, `3-axis acceleration`.
- A very short touch-and-shake test: the same touch triggers reliably; body movement produces different sonic changes.

**Narration (VO)**

> Robin is a performance system I soldered and programmed myself. It was born in a music-technology workshop: the hardware and the code, built with my own hands.
> It has eleven touch pads, but it can only read contact or no contact: zero or 127.
> It also reads my body: acceleration in three directions.
> Every touch has to be translated into data. Every movement, too.
> I could have tried to hide this translation, to make it feel like a precise, obedient MIDI controller.
> But I decided to leave the loss in the work.
> When the system can't fully understand my gesture, how do I keep making music with it?
> Or rather: where is the edge of what we can control?

**Fact check**

- Show only the hardware capability that already exists; never present unbuilt sound mappings as done.
- The origin wording is generic but true: the workshop (Creativitas) is real; no fabricated details.
- The "version rules" in the next section must be implemented before this film can be completed.

### Section 4. Intention Written as a Mapping: Who Controls What? (1:40–2:15)

**Image / sound**

- A minimal animated diagram, lighting up with real operations:

```text
my musical intention → measurable touch / movement → Robin's mapping → sonic result
        ↑                                                        │
        └──────────────── my next response, after hearing the result ─┘
```

- Then three demonstrations, each under ten seconds: steady trigger, slight movement, obvious movement. Each one isolates a single variable.

**Narration (VO)**

> So in this performance, I don't treat control and randomness as opposites.
> First I have to decide: which part do I hand to the system?
> I set which sounds can appear, and which rhythms must stay. But these rules are only a reproduction of expression, not expression itself.
> When a result spills out of the rule, I have to listen first, and then make my next move.
> I no longer try to eliminate the uncontrollable. I coexist with randomness, and let the chaos jam with me.

**On-screen text (final Robin mapping)**

| My intention | The proxy rule Robin receives | What the rule leaves open |
|---|---|---|
| Build a recognizable rhythm | Pads 1–4 trigger the rhythmic skeleton (drums + the count "one, two, three, four") | Subtle timbres, reverb, and the beat relationships rewritten later |
| Summon a certain kind of sound | Pads 5–8 choose from the voice pool (my own recorded instructions: "again" / "yes" / "no" / "stop"…) | Which phrase gets drawn, how far it is pitched or time-shifted, when it enters |
| Turn the structure, or leave space | Pads 9–11 trigger transitions / interruptions | The specific texture of the interruption, its tail, and the memory it leaves |
| Press in, widen possibility | X/Y/Z acceleration shapes density, bounded pitch drift, and spatial texture | The exact combination and timing of each change |
| Decide how far it can drift | CC0 bounds the system's range of activity | Results produced together by rules, probability, and memory within that range |

**Production notes**

- Uncertainty must be bounded. Random values only move inside pre-defined pitch ranges, sample pools, density, and FX limits. Otherwise the viewer hears glitches, not relationships.
- The voice pool must be recognizable at least once. The D2 count is that moment. Beyond it, fragments may be transformed, but their source must already have been remembered by the viewer in D2; otherwise the fragments sound like noise, not an intention distorted by a rule.
- Recording language of the samples: primarily English, with a little Chinese allowed. English keeps the samples legible to a general viewer; the few Chinese fragments work as a mark of my own practice habits, always subtitled, and never breaking the recognizability of the D2 count.

### Section 5. Performance: From Command to Negotiation (2:15–4:15)

**How the interpretation works**

These 120 seconds are not "perform first, explain later." At every key change, the viewer receives three things at once: my action, the system's current state, and one sentence that only changes how they listen. The narration never answers "what does this mean." While the result is still in their ears, it points at one relation: **what rule was my intention written into? What did the rule just leave out? What am I about to do in response?**

Editing density is the fourth piece of information: the cut rate rises steadily from D1 to D5 and falls back in D6. The viewer moves from slow, steady, predictable, to dense, fast, out of control, and is then brought back to a quiet close. The chaos comes from the editing; the reality comes from a single unbroken audio take and a continuous timeline.

**Sync interpretation beats**

| Moment | On-screen text / narration | The relation the viewer should read |
|---|---|---|
| D1 | "I set the boundary, but I can't decide every result inside it." | Intention is first written as a playable range. |
| D2 | "First, I decide what can happen." | A clear motif is the reference point for all later drift. |
| D3 | "I can choose the material, but I can't command when it answers." | Touch is a proxy rule, not a command for a result. |
| D4 | "The same gesture no longer returns to the same place." | The system's memory lets the past enter the present. |
| D5 | The screen highlights `intention → rule → drift` in sequence. No narration. | Drift has happened; the viewer must listen, not be told the answer. |
| D6 | "I didn't make it go back to how it was. I let what already happened change the next touch." | Authorship lives in the response, not in owning every result in advance. |

#### A. Four rules every "uncontrollable" event must satisfy

An event worthy of the word "uncontrollable" must meet all four conditions:

1. **Visible cause.** The viewer first sees my touch or movement, then hears the change it causes. The change never comes from nowhere.
2. **Not precisely rehearsable.** When I act, I only know that some class of events becomes more likely or some range widens. I do not know which exact sound, which moment, which pitch will appear.
3. **Has consequences.** Once an event appears, it lasts at least 1–3 seconds. It is not instantly muted or cut away. It enters the next musical state.
4. **Still inside bounds.** Volume, frequency, pitch, and density have ceilings. Uncontrollable is not chaos and not malfunction; the viewer should hear a sound that asks for a response, not a noise that cannot be parsed.

#### B. System states this performance uses (shared by Robin and the visualization)

| State | What it means | Who can change it | How the viewer sees it |
|---|---|---|---|
| `pulse` | whether the rhythmic skeleton is still clear | I build, pause, or rewrite it with Pads 1–4 | a four-cell rhythm grid, bottom-left; even white pulses when stable |
| `aperture` | how far the system is allowed to drift | I turn CC0 | a translucent band, center frame; wider means more possibility |
| `pressure` | how hard the body is pressing on the system | my X/Y/Z acceleration | motion traces and particle density; it is not directly any one sound |
| `memory` | the system's not-yet-faded earlier choices | shared between me and the system | a time trace on the right; old events fade but still shape the next choice |

**Suggested core logic:** my touch proposes a sonic intention; `pressure` raises the chance the system drifts from it; `memory` ties the next result to sounds that already happened; `aperture` bounds how far any drift can go. Uncontrollability then comes from constrained probability plus temporal feedback, not from a random button.

#### C. The roles of image and editing

| Layer | Question it must answer | Working rule |
|---|---|---|
| Live performer camera | Who is acting? What does the body do? | Causality stays visible at every system response: action first, sound after. Camera positions may switch freely. |
| Robin overhead | Which pad / knob is triggered? | Cut in only at key actions; after every close-up, return to the action or the result. |
| Real-time visualization | What state is the system in? | Show only the four states plus one continuous timeline. It is a score, not background decoration. |
| Subtitles / narration | What question should the viewer listen with? | Do not explain the meaning of the sound. Only establish a way of listening at turning points. |
| Cutting rate | How much is out of control? | The cut rate is a dramaturgical parameter: slow in D1–D2, mixing in D3, rising in D4, at its peak in D5, settling into a near long take in D6. |

- **Fixed color grammar:** white = events I trigger directly; one accent color = events the system generates or transforms inside its bounds; gray residue = memory. Colors never change randomly.
- **Montage discipline:** the image may cut at high frequency, but the sound is one continuous take, never edited; the visualization's timeline is always continuous, so the viewer can see that time never jumps. Montage is a rise in the density of perspectives, not a repair of facts.

#### D. Segment-by-segment performance score (about 120 seconds total)

**D1. Set the boundary / the viewer's contract (2:15–2:27, 12 s)**

- **Performer intent:** not "show off," but hand the viewer a recognizable possible world. I decide which sounds are allowed and how far the system may drift. Body stays still; CC0 at its narrowest.
- **Action and sound:** face Robin, pause about a second; slowly turn CC0 from zero to a low value. No complex material yet, only a low pulse or very light room tone.
- **Possible system response:** `aperture` opens from zero into a narrow band; the system generates no extra events. This moment must be almost fully controllable, or later drift has no reference point.
- **Narration** (one line only, before the performance truly begins): "Next, I'm going to set the boundary. But I can't decide every result inside it." It is a viewing instruction, not a philosophical conclusion.
- **Image and editing:** mostly wide, slow cuts; a very narrow `aperture` band overlaid; one CC0 close-up. Lowest cut rate of the film.
- **What the viewer should get:** there are rules here, and parts the rules cannot cover.

**D2. Build order / give the viewer a motif to remember (2:27–2:49, 22 s)**

- **Performer intent:** build a rhythmic identity someone can remember, using my own voice. The D2 motif is a recognizable count laid on top of the drum groove (subtitled "one, two, three, four"; the count may be recorded in Chinese as a natural home for a little Chinese). This is what I will lose, deform, and catch again.
- **Action and sound:** at 76–84 BPM, 4/4, Pads 1–4 build a loop of no more than four voices: a low hit, a clear high hit, a metallic / resonant color, and a short sound that appears only at the end of the phrase. No vocals, long delays, or large reverb at the start.
- **Possible system response:** only sounds I trigger. If any randomness survives, it stays at timbral details that cannot change the rhythmic identity. `memory` begins recording but does not intervene.
- **My response:** if the motif is not clear, remove a voice rather than add an effect. The success criterion is "the viewer can hear repetition," not "it sounds complex."
- **Narration / caption:** no narration; keep the sound. At the fifth second, one caption: "First, I decide what can happen." It disappears.
- **Image and editing:** mostly wide; a few overhead shots marking the four pads. The visualization shows only white, regular `pulse`; `aperture` is still narrow; `pressure` near zero. Low cut rate.
- **What the viewer should feel:** "He has it." At the same time, the viewer starts to predict the next beat. That prediction is exactly what will be broken later.

**D3. Press in / invite the drift (2:49–3:11, 22 s)**

- **Performer intent:** give the system more room to speak while keeping the rhythmic skeleton. The point is not "letting go"; it is testing whether uncertainty can live inside a structure that still makes music.
- **Action and sound:** trigger the voice pool on Pads 5–8 (my own recorded instructions: "again," "yes," "no," "stop," with a few Chinese fragments allowed); then only small, visible X-axis movement. The slices are 0.3–1 second fragments, never full sentences. Breathing space between movements.
- **Possible system response:** the X-axis raises the probability and density of fragments. The system may (1) drop a slice into the next bar's gap, (2) pull another phrase from the same class, or (3) do nothing at all for a moment. The third option matters: it makes probability, not button logic, believable.
- **My response:** if a slice lands in the gap, leave it and let it be fully heard. If it lands on a hit, move the next drum hit back half a beat or drop a voice. Never pretend it did not happen.
- **Narration / caption:** after the first movement, one quiet line: "I can choose the material, but I can't command when it answers." Then stop, so the first real response is not covered.
- **Image and editing:** first the shoulder and hand moving, then cut to the visualization: a `pressure` trace appears, accent-colored fragments pop out of the narrow band. Pad touches stay white. The cut rate begins to mix: wide + the first system visualization.
- **What the viewer should feel:** the movement and the change are related, but I cannot translate them one-to-one. The system is not a simple effects unit.

**D4. Memory intervenes / the same gesture no longer gives the same result (3:11–3:37, 26 s)**

- **Performer intent:** prove that uncontrollability is not "one random draw." The system keeps working with the sounds that came before. I am not facing a blank interface but a state already changed by a shared history.
- **Action and sound:** repeat similar touches and movement from D3, add slow Y-axis movement. The rhythm motif holds, reduced to two or three voices to make room. The voice fragments begin to be transposed, delayed, and overlapped: the viewer can hear that this is not the same line from before.
- **Possible system response:** `memory` lets the recent slices, gaps, and states shape the next choice; the Y-axis allows only small / moderate pitch offsets inside a fixed scale. The system may stretch a familiar slice to a different pitch, delay its entry, or overlap it with a residual tail.
- **My response:** this is where the first real "listening pause" must happen. Do not keep triggering. Listen for one or two seconds, then choose: restate a fragment of the original motif, or hand the center to the sound that just appeared. If the result is too full, touch less, but do not shut everything down.
- **Narration / caption:** no narration. After a similar gesture, one caption: "The same gesture no longer returns to the same place." Let the viewer hear why.
- **Image and editing:** gray `memory` residue gathers on the right of the visualization; new accent events grow out of the residue rather than appearing from nowhere. One shot of me stopping and listening toward Robin: a profile, a still hand. Cut rate rising.
- **What the viewer should feel:** the system has a history, and he has to keep playing inside it. This is the turning point of the performance: from manipulating an object to negotiating with a state.

**D5. Widen the opening / a drift that cannot be taken back (3:37–3:57, 20 s)**

- **Performer intent:** take the risk on purpose. Turn CC0 up, raise the movement range, not to make more noise, but to test how much drift the order can carry.
- **Action and sound:** push CC0 to mid-high; add an obvious Z-axis movement and one Pad 9–11 transition / interruption. The original count keeps only one or two basic hits; the voice pool is thoroughly fragmented, time-stretched, displaced, elongated.
- **Possible system response:** the Z-axis opens deeper space / delay / granular texture; the system may pick one or two moves from a bounded set: elongate a vocal tail, offset a set of beats, pull pitch apart inside a fixed scale, let residue form a temporary harmony. It must never transform the material past recognition.
- **My response:** this is a decision point, not a pre-scripted climax. If a usable long tail or harmony appears, stop adding material and hold it with a sparse drum hit. If the result is too dense, turn CC0 down, but let the tail that already exists ring to its end. Both responses say the same thing: I cannot undo what happened, only rewrite what happens next.
- **Narration / caption:** no narration. At most two words: "intention" as I turn CC0, "drift" after the system responds. Silence leaves room for the sound.
- **Image and editing:** the montage peak. High-frequency cuts: overhead pads, the live visualization, face close-ups, wide fragments. The cut rate reaches the film's maximum. `aperture` widens; accent traces spread but stay inside the frame. Note: the sound is one continuous take; the montage cuts image only, and the timeline never breaks.
- **What the viewer should feel:** not "the machine lost control," but "he built a situation he could not fully manage and still has to take responsibility for."

**D6. Respond and close / write the accident into the ending (3:57–4:15, 18 s)**

- **Performer intent:** do not return to the original pure order, and do not let the chaos simply fade. Use the drift that just happened as material and write a new, smaller ending.
- **Action and sound:** pause for half a beat to a beat; pick one feature from the most recognizable accident (a vocal tail, an offset rhythm, a new harmony) and answer it with the original Pads 1–4 motif. CC0 narrows slowly but never returns to zero. The count motif returns in an altered form: different attack, timbre, or space from D2. Leave one unresolved tail.
- **Possible system response:** `pressure` drops, the system generates less; `memory` still holds the recent result, so the final motif returns with changed timbre or rhythm. The system may add one small, unpredictable reverb tail, but it must not steal the ending.
- **My response:** do not try to "fix" it. The last touch admits that the change is already inside the work: the original motif returns, but its attack, timbre, or silence is different from D2.
- **Narration / caption:** only after the last sound, one line: "I didn't make it go back to how it was. I let what already happened change the next touch." This is the conclusion of the performance section, not of the film.
- **Image and editing:** the montage settles. The last five seconds return to a near long take of Robin and my hands; the visualization keeps a white motif and one accent residue side by side. Do not end with fast cuts or an exaggerated glitch.
- **What the viewer should carry away:** authorship did not disappear. It moved from deciding every result in advance to consciously responding to generated results.

#### E. Division of labor: narration, interview, and sound

| Element | Best placement | Its job | What it must not do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Narration** | at the edges of D1, D3, D6 | while the result is still happening, point at one relation in "intention–rule–drift–response" | describe every action, or announce for the viewer that "this is creative" |
| **Interview monologue** | after Robin's introduction, or after D3's first fully heard system response | say why this tension belongs to me: percussion training pursues accuracy, while composing forces me to face drift | cut in before D3's causality is established, or cover the key sounds |
| **Subtitles** | at most one line per section of the performance | give an abstract sound the smallest reading cue | type the conceptual essay onto the screen line by line |
| **The music itself** | most of D2–D5 | become the main evidence for the argument | be covered by continuous voice, score, or visual effects |

**Interview monologue (8–10 seconds), recommended before D1** so the viewer enters the performance with a personal question:

> When I studied percussion, I practiced to make every hit land more precisely.
> But when I started writing my own music, I found that some of the sounds I treasure most came exactly from the moments that refused to go as planned.

If a truly synchronized slice is needed, once D3's first system response has been fully heard, lay no more than 3 seconds of voice over the performance image:

> What I give it is the trajectory of a gesture, not what I want it to sound like.

It does not start a new section and does not cut away from the performance. It only names the causality after the viewer has already heard it.

#### F. Rehearsal acceptance checklist

After one full rehearsal, check each item:

1. Without the narration, can the viewer hear the D2 count motif, and hear in D4/D5 that it actually changed?
2. Can the viewer point to at least one moment of "his action triggered a change he could not predict"?
3. Does every system event still have a musical boundary, rather than sounding like an effect or a bug?
4. Does the D6 ending carry traces of D5, rather than a simple reset to the opening?
5. Without the visualization, does the performance still hold? With it, does it actually make the causality clearer?
6. Does the cutting rate complete the arc from dense to calm, rather than random fast cutting?
7. After the montage, can the viewer still tell that the music is one continuous take? (Have an uninformed viewer answer: was the music edited?)

- The image pulls back from a near shot of Robin and my hands to a fixed, farther frame. The live state readout keeps only the white motif and one accent residue.

> But it's in the music now, and it's in my next choice too.

This layer is not spelled out. The fragment's meaning must already hold in the sound (established in D2). If it is not audible, let it exist as pure sound; never patch the concept with text.

## 3. What Robin Must Deliver Before Shooting

Without these, the performance logic in the script does not hold.

1. **A stable four-voice rhythmic skeleton.** Pads 1–4 must trigger reliably and repeat under low movement, becoming the viewer's reference for "order."
2. **The acceleration axes separated.** X, Y, Z must not control everything at once; each axis controls one of probability / bounded pitch drift / spatial or granular texture, so the camera can show causality.
3. **A boundary knob.** CC0 directly controls the range of randomness, drift, or FX ceiling, becoming the visible action of tightening or loosening the system.
4. **The voice pool and the rhythm pool.** Record 6–12 spoken instructions ("again," "yes," "no," "stop," etc.), primarily English with a few Chinese fragments allowed, uniform in volume, length, and treatment; record a recognizable count motif "one, two, three, four" (which may be recorded in Chinese) as the D2 identity; add my own drum and metronome material. Neither pool may overpower the rhythmic skeleton the moment it is triggered.
5. **Random-seed or parameter logging.** The final performance need not be exactly reproducible, but rehearsal must be able to locate which rule produced which sound, so it can be changed and shot.
6. **The real-time visualization:** the four states (`pulse`, `aperture`, `pressure`, `memory`) plus one continuous timeline. It runs in real time with Robin during the performance. It is simultaneously the evidence for the system's state and the anchor proving time is unedited, not background decoration. The four states must share the same state data as Robin's code, so what the viewer sees and hears come from the same run.

**Not a priority:** Max/MSP or FL Studio, unless Tone.js cannot implement a required mapping; more sensors or pads (what is missing is an audible set of rules, not input count); heavy real-time visuals (the visualization only needs to help the viewer read the system).
